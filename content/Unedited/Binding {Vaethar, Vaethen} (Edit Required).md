# I. What “creating a binding” means (core concept)

A **binding** (carrier) is not:

- a feeling
    
- an intention
    
- a promise in the casual sense
    

In Isathes, a binding exists only if **all three conditions are met**:

> **Alignment + Declaration + Endurance**

If any of these are missing, no carrier exists.

---

### 1. Alignment (IVb: will / obligation)

Someone must **stand in will** toward the binding.

- _unel_ — to will
    
- _vaenel_ — to be obligated
    

But IVb **never creates a binding on its own**.

---

### 2. Declaration (Class I or II)

There must be an **externalized act**:

- an utterance
    
- a rite
    
- a witnessed action
    
- an institutional designation
    

This is where bindings _enter the world_.

---

### 3. Endurance (Class III)

The result must **persist** beyond the moment.

This is why bindings are carriers:  
they exist _as long as they endure_.

---

### Canon definition (save this)

> **A binding is a result state created by an act of declaration aligned with will, which persists independently of the moment of creation and may constrain participants via `se`.**

That sentence explains the whole system.

---

# II. Canonical ways a binding is created

Bindings are **not generic**. Isathes culture distinguishes _how_ they come into being.

Here are the **canonical creation paths**, all of which produce **Class I result nominals** (_vaethar / vaethen_).

---

## A. Declarative Binding (Speech-based)

### Mechanism

- **Class I utterance verb** (_isaeth_)
    
- plus IVb alignment (_unel_)
    
- in a recognized context
    

### Grammar sketch

`[binding] ve isaeth   (aligned with unel) → produces vaethar / vaethen`

### Cultural meaning

- oaths
    
- vows
    
- sworn promises
    
- named obligations
    

This is where **speech becomes dangerous**.

---

## B. Ritual Binding (Act-based)

### Mechanism

- Class I physical or symbolic act
    
- witnesses or ritual space
    
- alignment assumed or declared
    

### Grammar sketch

`[binding] ve [ritual-eth] → produces vaethar`

### Cultural meaning

- marriages
    
- blood-oaths
    
- initiation rites
    
- curses and wards
    

Often stronger than speech alone.

---

## C. Institutional Binding (Designation-based)

### Mechanism

- **Class II designation** (_ordar_)
    
- recognition by an authority
    

### Grammar sketch

`[office] ve ordar → ordarthir (institutional carrier)`

Then:

`X se ordarthir`

### Cultural meaning

- offices
    
- duties
    
- ranks
    
- imposed obligations
    

These bindings persist **without the individual’s will** once accepted.

---

## D. Imposed Binding (Asymmetric)

### Mechanism

- Class I causative act
    
- alignment only on one side (or none)
    

### Grammar sketch

`[binding] ve vaeth → vaethar`

### Cultural meaning

- coercion
    
- punishment
    
- enslavement
    
- curses
    

These are culturally recognized as **violent bindings**.

---

# III. Explicit Class I release verbs (ending a binding by act)

You are correct: **cessation alone is not always release**.

Sometimes:

- the binding ends naturally (na-vesal)
    
- sometimes it is **actively undone**
    

These must be **Class I**, because they are acts.

---

## Canon release verbs (proposed semantic set)

You don’t need many; fewer is better.

### 1. _to release / unbind_ (neutral, mutual)

- Ends a binding cleanly
    
- Often consensual
    

Grammar role:

`[binding] ve [release-eth] → binding no longer constrains`

---

### 2. _to renounce / annul_ (authoritative)

- Ends a binding by authority
    
- Often institutional
    

Used with:

- offices
    
- doctrines
    
- imposed oaths
    

---

### 3. _to sever / break_ (violent)

- Ends a binding by force
    
- May carry consequences
    

Often paired with:

`[binding] ve na-vesal`

To emphasize destruction of endurance.

---

### Canon statement (release verbs)

> **Explicit release verbs are Class I acts which terminate obligation carriers directly. They differ from cessation constructions in that they assert an intentional act of dissolution rather than the result alone.**

This keeps:

- cessation = result-focused
    
- release = action-focused

# IV. “Willingly forsake” (IVb — not release)

This is an _excellent_ instinct, and you’re right:  
**forsaking is not the same as releasing.**

---

## What “forsake” actually is in Isathes

To forsake is:

- a change in **alignment**
    
- a refusal to continue standing in will
    
- **not** the end of the binding itself
    

So this must be **Class IVb**.

---

## Canon behavior

- Forsaking:
    
    - changes the speaker’s will
        
    - may _violate_ a binding
        
    - does **not** dissolve the carrier
        

Grammar-wise:

- no object
    
- no Ve
    
- no past tense
    
- expresses misalignment
    

---

### Cultural meaning

Forsaking:

- may incur penalty
    
- may invoke enforcement
    
- may transform the binding (e.g. curse, debt)
    

This is socially explosive language.

---

### Canon contrast (important)

|Act|Binding exists after?|
|---|---|
|Cessation (na-vesal)|❌ No|
|Release (Class I)|❌ No|
|Forsake (IVb)|✅ Yes|

That distinction is gold.

# V. Canon summary (drop-in)

Here’s a tight block you can save:

> **Bindings in Isathes are created through acts of declaration, ritual, or designation aligned with will, producing enduring carriers that constrain participants via `se`. Bindings persist independently of continued alignment. They may end by cessation, by explicit release acts (Class I), or persist despite forsaking (Class IVb), which alters alignment but does not dissolve the binding itself.**

## I. Oath-Specific Bindings

### 1. What an oath _is_ in Isathes

An oath is **not just a binding**.

It is a binding with **self-asserted origin**.

Canon definition:

> **An oath is a binding whose creation explicitly asserts the will (`unel`) of the one constrained as part of its formation.**

That single clause is what distinguishes oaths from:

- imposed bindings
    
- institutional obligations
    
- coercive bonds
    

---

### 2. Oath carrier (linguistic category)

Oaths are a **subclass of obligation carriers**, with additional properties.

They are:

- Class I result nominals (like other bindings)
    
- almost always **concrete** (`-ethar` rather than `-ethen`)
    
- culturally marked as _self-anchored_
    

You do **not** need a new grammatical class, just a recognized subtype.

---

### 3. Creation of an oath (canonical)

An oath exists only if all three are present:

1. **Declared binding** (Class I act, usually utterance)
    
2. **Explicit alignment** (IVb will asserted or presupposed)
    
3. **Enduring carrier** produced
    

If any of these is missing:

- the utterance may still exist
    
- the promise may be socially meaningful
    
- but **no oath exists grammatically**
    

This prevents accidental oaths.

---

## II. Forsaking vs Breaking (this is the moral core)

These are **not synonyms**, and Isathes forces speakers to choose.

---

### A. Forsaking an oath (IVb alignment failure)

#### What it is

- A **withdrawal of will**
    
- A refusal to continue standing aligned
    
- A declaration of misalignment
    

#### What it is _not_

- It does **not** end the oath
    
- It does **not** dissolve the binding
    
- It does **not** remove consequences
    

#### Linguistic profile

- Class IVb construction
    
- No object
    
- No `Ve`
    
- No past tense
    

Canon framing:

> **Forsaking alters alignment but leaves the oath intact.**

This is crucial.

---

#### Cultural implication

- Forsaking is **admission of breach**
    
- It invites judgment, punishment, or enforcement
    
- It is often considered _worse_ than breaking by force, because it is voluntary
    

---

### B. Breaking an oath (carrier destruction)

#### What it is

- The **destruction or cessation of the oath carrier**
    
- Usually via:
    
    - explicit release (Class I)
        
    - or violent severance
        
    - or ontological termination
        

#### Linguistic profile

- Uses cessation or release constructions
    
- Carrier is the grammatical target
    

Example pattern (schematic):

`X se oath oath ve na-vesal`

#### Canon framing:

> **Breaking an oath ends the binding, regardless of alignment.**

---

#### Cultural implication

- Breaking is an **act**
    
- It carries responsibility
    
- It may still be condemned, but it is _structurally honest_
    

Isathes culture tends to judge:

- _forsaking_ as moral failure
    
- _breaking_ as violent necessity or crime
    

---

## III. Interaction with Death, Exile, and Boundary Exclusion

This is where your **thesal / kisal / vesal** distinctions pay off.

---

### A. Death (ontological termination)

#### Case 1: Ordinary death

`X ve na-thesal`

What happens to oaths?

- The **person no longer exists**
    
- But the **oath carrier may still exist**
    

Result:

- The oath no longer constrains X
    
- But it may still bind:
    
    - descendants
        
    - institutions
        
    - consequences (curses, debts, reputations)
        

Death ends **alignment**, not necessarily **carriers**.

---

#### Case 2: Oath bound “beyond life”

If the oath’s creation explicitly asserts endurance beyond thesal:

- death does **not** end constraint
    
- the carrier remains active in other domains
    

This is rare and terrifying — appropriately so.

---

### B. Exile (boundary exclusion)

#### Linguistic structure

`Nare [domain], X na-kisal`

or, more severely:

`Nare [domain], X na-thesal`

#### Effect on oaths

- Exile does **not destroy** the oath
    
- It limits **where** it applies
    

The oath still exists,  
but no longer applies _within_ the excluded domain.

This produces statements like:

> “You are no longer bound **here**, but the oath endures.”

That distinction is extremely powerful socially.

---

### C. Boundary exclusion of the oath itself

If the oath carrier is excluded from a domain:

`Nare [domain], oath na-kisal`

Meaning:

- the oath does not apply _here_
    
- but may apply elsewhere
    

This is how:

- sanctuaries
    
- neutral grounds
    
- ritual spaces  
    function linguistically.

## Canon paragraph you can drop in whole

> **Oaths in Isathes are self-asserted bindings created through declared acts aligned with will. Forsaking an oath withdraws alignment but leaves the binding intact, constituting a breach rather than a release. Breaking an oath destroys or ends the binding itself. Death ends alignment but does not necessarily dissolve oath carriers, while exile and boundary exclusion limit the domains in which oaths apply rather than ending them outright.**
